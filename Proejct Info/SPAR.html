Four Eras of Scenario Planning: From Intuition to Argument

The same question — "what could go wrong, and what would it do to us?" — answered with progressively better tools. Each era is traced through its core method, algorithm, or mathematics, the limitation it left behind, and the era it forced into being. SPAR is the fourth.

Era 1 · 1960s–1990s

Narrative scenario planning

The question answered by structured human imagination.

In plain words

Picture the very first scenario planners as smart people sitting in a room, imagining the future. The military did it for war planning — "thinking the unthinkable" — and then Shell borrowed it for business: "What if oil prices crash? Let's write out that story now and prepare for it."

Their superpower was human imagination. The catch: it was just words — no number a bank could act on, slow to produce, and heavily shaped by whoever happened to be in the room. That gap, "stop describing and give me a figure," is exactly what pushed the field into the next era.

Scenario planning began not in finance but in strategy and defence. Herman Kahn pioneered "thinking the unthinkable" at the RAND Corporation; Pierre Wack adapted it into a corporate discipline at Royal Dutch Shell, whose Scenarios: Uncharted Waters Ahead [1] remains canonical. Wack's central claim is the seed of SPAR: multiple, conflicting viewpoints are required to defeat group-think. Peter Schoemaker later turned the art into a repeatable procedure [2].

Scenario-axis construction (logic, not equations)
1. rank driving forces by impact × uncertainty → pick the 2 most critical, most uncertain
2. cross them as axes → 2 × 2 = 4 internally-consistent future worlds
Supported by the Delphi method (anonymous expert rounds revised toward convergence) and cross-impact analysis (a matrix of how each event shifts the probability of every other). Output = rich narrative worlds, not numbers.
Limitation → Purely verbal. No loss numbers, slow, and dependent on who was in the room. Finance needed a number it could put on a balance sheet.

Era 2 · 1990s–2010s

Quantitative stress testing

The question answered by probability and linear algebra.

In plain words

Finance looked at those rich stories and said: "Nice — but give me a number." So it built math tools that crunch decades of market history to answer one question: "How much could I lose, and how likely is that?" Value-at-Risk gives the loss line you'd rarely cross; Monte Carlo rolls the dice on thousands of possible futures; factor models translate a macro move into a per-sector hit.

After the 2008 crash, regulators made this mandatory — big banks now must run official stress tests (CCAR/DFAST in the US). The strength was precision. The weakness: these tools only learn from the past, so a brand-new kind of shock the data has never seen catches them off guard. The problem to fix: blind to anything truly novel — and still authored by a single source with nobody to argue back.

Finance made scenarios numerical. The foundations were Markowitz's mean-variance theory [3] and the CAPM [4], extended by the Fama–French factor model [5] — the direct ancestor of SPAR's Layer 3.

Factor models — the engine SPAR reuses

CAPM & Fama–French three-factor
E[Rᵢ] = R_f + βᵢ(E[R_m] − R_f) , βᵢ = Cov(Rᵢ,R_m) / Var(R_m)
Rᵢ−R_f = αᵢ + βᵢ(R_m−R_f) + sᵢ·SMB + hᵢ·HML + εᵢ
β, s, h are sensitivities estimated by regressing a sector's excess returns on the market, size (SMB) and value (HML) factors. Plug in a scenario's factor moves → get a predicted sector return. This is exactly how SPAR's Layer 3 turns an agreed scenario into per-sector losses.

Risk measures — VaR, EWMA volatility, Expected Shortfall

Value-at-Risk, volatility updating, tail risk
VaR_α = inf{ l : P(L > l) ≤ 1−α } ≈ μ − z_α·σ
σ²ₜ = λ·σ²ₜ₋₁ + (1−λ)·r²ₜ₋₁ , λ = 0.94  (EWMA)
ES_α = E[ L | L ≥ VaR_α ]  (Expected Shortfall)
Popularised by J.P. Morgan's RiskMetrics [6]. z_α is the normal quantile (1.65 at 95%). Expected Shortfall — also reported by SPAR's Layer 3 — averages the losses beyond the cutoff, fixing VaR's blind spot.

Simulation & regulatory stress tests

Monte Carlo path (geometric Brownian motion)
Sₜ₊Δ = Sₜ · exp[ (μ − ½σ²)Δ + σ√Δ · Z ] , Z ~ N(0,1)
After 2008 the Fed's CCAR / DFAST [7] made this mandatory — banks receive fixed adverse macro paths and must project losses, typically via regressions of loss on macro variables.
Limitation → Every estimate is calibrated on history. A structurally novel shock is mispriced, and the scenario is still authored by a single source with no challenge.
▼   let machines read the world
Era 3 · 2018–2023

AI, NLP & financial language models

The question answered by models that read text, not just tables.

In plain words

Then AI got good at language. Earlier machine learning was already quietly running behind the scenes — scoring loan applications, catching fraud — but only on neat tables of numbers. The leap here was that models like FinBERT and BloombergGPT could read the messy text of the world — news, earnings calls, filings — and judge its mood, something pure number-crunching could never do.

Now a machine could process not just prices, but the narrative around them. The catch that remained: it was still one model giving one answer, confidently, with no way to question itself or show the reasoning behind its verdict.

Machine learning first spread through structured-data tasks — credit scoring, fraud, algorithmic trading — typically with logistic regression and gradient-boosted trees:

Logistic model for default / fraud probability
P(default | x) = σ(wᵀx) , σ(z) = 1 / (1 + e^−z)
A weighted sum of features squashed to a 0–1 probability — still the workhorse of credit risk.

The Transformer turn

Self-attention (Vaswani et al.)
Attention(Q,K,V) = softmax( QKᵀ / √d_k ) V
Q, K, V = query, key, value projections. The dot-product scores how much each token attends to every other; softmax turns scores into weights. This is the engine under BERT [9] and every LLM since [8].

Financial language models

FinBERT [10] fine-tuned BERT on financial text; BloombergGPT [11] (50B params, 363B-token corpus) and open FinGPT [12] brought generative financial NLP:

Training objectives
MLM (BERT): maximise Σ log P(x_masked | x_context)
Autoregressive (GPT): maximise Σₜ log P(xₜ | x_<t ; θ)
Fine-tune classifier: minimise L = −Σ_c y_c log ŷ_c  (cross-entropy)
Lopez-Lira & Tang [13] showed even an off-the-shelf LLM predicts short-horizon returns from headlines — evidence LLMs encode usable financial reasoning.
Limitation → Powerful, but still one model, one answer. No mechanism to interrogate itself, surface a missed channel, or preserve dissent.
▼   make them reason, and argue
Era 4 · 2023–now

Agentic AI & multi-agent debate

The question answered by a society of reasoning agents — where SPAR lives.

In plain words

The newest leap: instead of one AI answering alone, you give several AIs different expert roles and let them argue. Researchers found that AIs which debate and critique each other reach better, more accurate answers than any single one of them does alone — disagreement flushes out mistakes.

Notice this quietly circles all the way back to Wack's 1985 insight — that clashing viewpoints prevent group-think — only now it runs with AI agents, at machine speed, with a written record of the whole argument. SPAR lives here, and adds one twist: rather than always debating a fixed number of rounds, it watches the discussion and stops the moment the agents stop learning anything new.

An agent uses an LLM as a reasoning core that perceives, plans, uses tools, reflects, and acts. ReAct [14] interleaves reasoning and action; Reflexion [15] adds verbal self-critique:

Agent control loops
ReAct: (thoughtₜ → actionₜ → observationₜ) repeated until goal
Reflexion: memoryₜ₊₁ = memoryₜ ∪ selfReflection(resultₜ)
Reflexion is single-agent self-critique. SPAR's Devil's Advocate externalises the same idea into a separate adversarial agent.

From one agent to a debating society

Surveys mapped autonomous agents [16]; Generative Agents [17] showed believable simulation; AutoGen [18] and CAMEL [19] made multi-agent conversation a framework. The key result is multi-agent debate [20]:

Multi-agent debate update (Du et al.)
aᵢ⁽ʳ⁺¹⁾ = LLM( q , { aⱼ⁽ʳ⁾ }j≠i ) for rounds r = 0…R
final = consensus / majority over { aᵢ⁽ᴿ⁾ }
In the original method each agent rewrites its answer after reading every other agent's answer, for several rounds. SPAR adapts this: specialist agents may read each other's positions, but are given zero power to remove, vote out, or aggregate one another. All critique flows only through the Devil's Advocate, and all merging only through the Moderator — so agents benefit from seeing other views, yet no agent can be argued out of the room. Liang et al. [21] show debate counters "degeneration of thought"; in finance, TradingAgents [23] (surveyed in [22]) applies bull/bear debate to trade decisions.
Debate Continuation Score (this work)
DCS = w₁·Disagreement + w₂·InfoGain + w₃·(1−RAGexhaustion)
if DCS > τ and round < 5 → explore else exploit
Disagreement = variance of severity ratings; InfoGain = how much positions moved; RAGexhaustion = how repetitive the cited sources have become.

Applying it · Layer 1 evaluation

How we judge the agents — rubrics, benchmarks & when to stop

Two yardsticks, not one

In plain words

Judge how well an agent reasons with the same ruler for everyone (fairness — nobody is punished for their domain). Judge whether an agent was factually right with a domain-specific ruler (the "right answer" for the Monetary agent differs from the Geopolitical one). Split these two and the confusion disappears.

Benchmarks for this layer

What to compare against
1 · The baseline. Single-model (no debate) vs. SPAR debate, on the same three scenario metrics (direction, magnitude, transmission completeness).
2 · Human expert agreement. A finance person scores sample transcripts; measure agreement with the moderator's scores via Cohen's κ.
3 · Ablations. Devil's-Advocate on/off, regime on/off, fixed vs adaptive rounds — does each component earn its place?
4 · Channel completeness. Agents' channels vs a ground-truth channel list with precision / recall / F1 (recall matters most).

Is a 5-round cap unfair? What does the threshold decide?

In plain words

The 5-round cap is just a safety net, not the brain. It exists for cost, the fact that LLM debates repeat after a few rounds, and reproducibility. State it openly as a limitation — that's good practice.

The real stop is the threshold τ on the Debate Continuation Score: stop when DCS < τ (argument went quiet) or round = 5, whichever first.

What τ decides — the explore/exploit knob
τ decides the trade-off between thoroughness and noise.
• High τ (strict) → stops early → cheaper, but risks ending before every channel surfaces.
• Low τ (lenient) → debates longer → thorough, but risks rounds that add only repetition or unjustified position-flips (can inflate a false consensus).
So τ marks the point where the value of one more round drops below its cost.
How to set τ defensibly → Run all five events to the full 5 rounds once, record the information-gain curve, and set τ at the empirical "elbow" where it flattens; report a sensitivity check at τ±ε. Same cap and τ for every event = fair. The round-count distribution then becomes a finding in its own right.

SPAR as a LangGraph StateGraph

The full Layer 0–3 pipeline drawn as a graph of nodes (tasks) and edges (flow). One shared memory passes through every node; the graph's shape decides who runs, when, and what they can see.

First, in plain words

Think of the system as an assembly line with one clipboard that travels down it. The clipboard is the shared State — it holds everything known so far (the shock, the regime, every agent's position, the scores).

Each node is a worker who picks up the clipboard, does one job, writes the result, and passes it on. The edges are the arrows. Some are straight ("always go here next"); some are decisions (a diamond that routes the clipboard based on what's written on it). Agents never talk directly — they only read and write the clipboard, and the graph decides the order.

Anchors

The two anchors: where it begins and ends

In plain words

Entry (START → Regime Classifier). The graph wakes the moment a shock is typed. The first worker doesn't judge the shock — it reads what kind of world it is landing in.

Finish (Report Builder → END). The line ends when the report is assembled. There's a second exit: if the Plausibility Gate finds the scenario too far-fetched, the clipboard is routed to a human instead — a safety stop.

Walkthrough

One run, start to finish

  1. A user types "US–Iran tanker strike spikes oil." The graph starts.
  2. Regime Classifier notes inflation is already high → a fragile world.
  3. Shock Profiler tags it "geopolitical-energy," wakes the 4 relevant specialists, writes their search hints.
  4. Debate Round 1: the 4 agents each give a view; the Devil's Advocate attacks the shakiest claims.
  5. Continuation Controller sees agents still disagree → explore → loops back for Round 2 (stops by Round 5 at the latest).
  6. Once converged, Moderator writes the consensus + preserved dissent + scores.
  7. Plausibility Gate finds it realistic → Quantifier produces sector losses + VaR.
  8. Report Builder assembles the final report → END.
The one rule that keeps it fair

Every node reads and writes the same shared State. The four specialists may read each other's notes, but have zero power to remove, vote out, or aggregate one another. All critique flows only through the Devil's Advocate; all merging only through the Moderator. That's what stops agents ganging up to silence a weaker one.
