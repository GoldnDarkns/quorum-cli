The Problem

Why existing stress tests fail

When a geopolitical shock occurs — a military strike, a sanctions announcement, an energy supply disruption — financial institutions need to know the market impact immediately. Today they cannot.

❌ What exists today

Fixed templates — calibrated to 2008, updated manually over weeks, cannot adapt to novel events.

Single-model AI — one LLM has one perspective, no mechanism to challenge its own assumptions, hallucinates with confidence.

No domain separation — political, economic, geopolitical, and market dynamics all lumped into one unstructured prompt.

No validation — nobody checks if the AI's scenario is plausible or historically grounded before it reaches a risk manager.

✓ What risk managers actually need

Speed — scenario in under 60 seconds, not weeks of manual analysis.

Multiple perspectives — political, economic, monetary, and market analysts all weighing in simultaneously.

Structured disagreement — where experts disagree preserved as tail-risk signals, not averaged away.

Auditable reasoning — every claim traceable to a data source or historical analogue, not a black-box output.

The core gap: no existing system combines real-time geopolitical shock intake, structured multi-agent debate across domain specialists, historical analogue retrieval, and validated portfolio impact estimation in a single auditable pipeline.

Research Question

Does a structured debate between domain-specialist LLMs from different model providers produce more accurate and complete financial stress scenarios than a single prompted model?

Hypothesis

Multi-agent debate across genuinely different frontier LLMs produces scenarios that are more directionally accurate, more complete in transmission channel coverage, and better calibrated in magnitude than a single model.

Why "different providers"

Claude, GPT-4o, Gemini, DeepSeek, and Grok have genuinely different training data, geographic biases, and reasoning tendencies. This diversity is real — not simulated through personas in one model.

How we prove it

Run five historical geopolitical events through both systems. Score outputs against actual S&P 500 outcomes on three rubrics. Compare scores. Report honestly — including failures.

Literature Review

Four streams of prior work

Stream 1 · Stress Testing

Traditional scenario generation

Basel III and CCAR established fixed shock frameworks. Brunnermeier et al. (2020) showed these fail under novel tail events. Static templates cannot handle geopolitical shocks whose form has never been seen before.

Stream 2 · Multi-Agent Debate

LLM debate reduces hallucination

Du et al. (2023) proved that multiple LLM instances critiquing each other improves factual accuracy and reduces overconfidence. FinDebate (2025) applied this to earnings analysis. Neither studied geopolitical shocks or used different model providers.

Stream 3 · Geopolitical Risk

GPR index and market transmission

Caldara & Iacoviello (2022) built the GPR index — the backbone of our retrieval. Ramiah, Xu & Moosa (2015) showed S&P 500 sector reactions vary significantly by shock category. Baker, Bloom & Davis (2016) documented policy uncertainty transmission channels.

Stream 4 · LLM + Finance

LLMs for financial analysis

BloombergGPT (2023) showed domain-specific training improves financial tasks. Soleimani et al. (2025) used RAG for G7 stress scenarios — acknowledged plausibility validation as unsolved. No paper uses multi-LLM debate for geopolitical scenario generation.

What all four streams miss: a system that combines structured debate, multiple model providers, geopolitical retrieval, and empirical validation against real historical market outcomes — for US financial markets specifically.
Research Gap

Three gaps — three contributions

01

No structured analogue retrieval for US geopolitical shocks

Existing papers either use fixed shock templates or retrieve analogues by keyword alone. Nobody has designed a three-dimensional matching system — by shock category, geographic proximity, and economic regime — grounded in the GPR index and FRED macro data. We build this and use it as our retrieval backbone.

02

Multi-agent debate never tested with genuinely different model providers

Du et al. and FinDebate use the same model prompted differently. We use Claude, GPT-4o, Gemini Pro, DeepSeek, and Grok — five companies with genuinely different training data, geographic emphases, and reasoning tendencies. We compare model-diversity debate vs. persona-diversity debate as a research experiment.

03

No validated plausibility framework for LLM-generated scenarios

Soleimani et al. (2025) explicitly acknowledge this as an open problem. We solve it with a three-pronged framework: historical analogue match, internal economic consistency, and benchmark comparison against Federal Reserve stress scenario language. We also introduce a four-type position-change decomposition as a debate quality metric.

Formal Problem Statement
Problem Statement

Financial institutions lack a rapid, auditable, and multi-perspective method for translating novel geopolitical shock narratives into structured, historically-grounded portfolio risk estimates. Existing approaches — static scenario templates and single-model AI generation — fail to capture the multi-domain nature of geopolitical transmission, produce unverifiable outputs, and cannot adapt to shock types unseen in their calibration data.

What we propose

SPAR — a four-layer agentic framework in which five frontier LLMs from different providers debate geopolitical shocks as domain specialists, grounded in historically retrieved analogues, with an adaptive explore/exploit stopping criterion, and validated against real S&P 500 sector outcomes through a structured three-metric scoring rubric.

Traditional stress testing asks: "What if 2008 happens again?"  ·  SPAR asks: "Given today's regime, through which channels would this shock move the portfolio?"
Architecture

Four-layer system overview

Data flows top to bottom. The user types a shock. The system classifies it, debates it, validates it, and quantifies it. Two conditional decision points control the flow — the Explore/Exploit loop and the Plausibility Gate.

Layer 0 · Classification

Regime classifier → 6-axis shock profiler → targeted RAG

Reads current market regime (6 conditions). Profiles the shock (7 types, historical analogues, transmission channels, GPR baseline). Routes only relevant agents. Seeds each agent's RAG with specific queries.

↓  targeted context packages → activated agents only
Layer 1 · Debate + Explore/Exploit

Specialist agents debate → adaptive stopping criterion

Active agents debate in structured rounds. After each round: Disagreement Score + Information Gain + RAG Exhaustion → Debate Continuation Score. If above threshold: explore (another round, max 5). If below: exploit (pass to Layer 2).

🏛🌐 Political & Geopolitical
📈💰 Economic, Fiscal & Market
🌿💻 Environmental & Technology
👥 Social & Behavioural
😈 Devil's Advocate
↓  full debate transcript → moderator
Layer 2 · Moderation + Validation

Consensus + minority dissent + plausibility gate

Separate moderator LLM synthesises debate into consensus scenario JSON and preserves minority views. Scores plausibility (0–100). Plausibility Gate: below threshold → human review; above → Layer 3.

↓  validated scenario JSON → impact model
Layer 3 · Quantification

Factor model → VaR → sector heatmap → report

Macro scenario variables mapped to S&P 500 sector sensitivities via calibrated factor model. Outputs: portfolio VaR, Expected Shortfall, sector P&L heatmap, confidence bands, plain-English narrative.

LangGraph Implementation

Why LangGraph and how it works

What LangGraph does

LangGraph is the orchestration framework that manages the multi-agent pipeline as a directed graph — controlling which agent runs when, how information flows between them, and when to loop vs. exit.

🗂 Shared State

Every agent reads and writes to a shared state object — the common whiteboard. Contains: shock description, regime profile, all agent positions per round, round counter, and final scenario. No agent can delete another's output.

📦 Nodes & Edges

Nodes = agents and decision logic. Straight edges = always proceed. Conditional edges = the two decision diamonds (Continuation Controller and Plausibility Gate). Only these two points can change the flow.

Flow diagram
START → Regime Classifier
→ Shock Profiler
→ Debate Round (4 domain agents)
→ Devil's Advocate
→ ◆ Continuation Controller
→ EXPLORE: loop ↑ back
→ EXPLOIT: Moderator
→ ◆ Plausibility Gate
→ LOW: Human Review → END
→ OK: Quantifier → Report → END

The explore/exploit loop in code

The Continuation Controller computes Disagreement Score + Information Gain + RAG Exhaustion → combined threshold τ. LangGraph's conditional edge branches on this: if score > τ AND rounds < 5, route back to Debate Round. Otherwise route to Moderator. This replaces fixed round count with adaptive stopping.

Implementation Plan · Scope

Narrowed for research rigour

We narrowed our scope to where data is most abundant, geography is well-covered by free APIs, and transmission chains are cleanly documentable for validation. Here are the decisions and the reasoning behind each.

Geography

United States · S&P 500

US markets have the deepest, cleanest, most API-accessible data globally. FRED, yfinance, GDELT, GPR — all free, all reliable, all covering US from the 1990s. Eliminates the data availability problem entirely.

Shock Type 1

Geopolitical military escalation

GPR index has documented every major military escalation since 1985. Transmission chain is well-studied: risk premium → VIX spike → dollar → defensive sectors. Abundant historical analogues.

Shock Type 2

Energy supply disruption

Crude oil supply shocks — well-documented via FRED oil series going back to 1970s. Clear sector differentiation: energy wins, airlines and consumer discretionary lose. Clean to validate.

Evaluation · Rubrics

How we score and compare

Three primary metrics. Scored for both Approach A (single LLM personas) and Approach B (multi-LLM debate) on all five events. Pre-registered before running any experiments.

🎯
Metric 1 — Directional Accuracy
Did the system correctly predict whether the S&P 500 (or key sector) moved up or down after the shock? Binary — 0 or 1 per sector per event. We check five sectors per event: Energy, Financials, Technology, Defence, Consumer Discretionary.
0 or 1 · binary
📐
Metric 2 — Magnitude Calibration
Was the estimated percentage move within a reasonable range of what actually happened? We define a tolerance band: within 20% of actual = 1 point, within 40% = 0.5 points, outside 40% = 0 points. Prevents the system from getting credit for directional accuracy on wildly wrong magnitudes.
0, 0.5, or 1
🔗
Metric 3 — Transmission Channel Completeness
What fraction of the actual transmission channels (documented in post-event academic literature) did the system identify? E.g., for Russia-Ukraine: did agents mention crude price, European growth slowdown, defence spending, INR pressure, and supply chain disruption? Each missed channel costs points.
0 to 1 · continuous
Position-change decomposition · debate quality
✓ Evidence-based revision — agent cites new data
⚠ Consensus pressure — agent changes because others did
– No meaningful update — same position, different words
✗ Unsupported flip — reversal with no reason given
Scoring approach

Each scenario scored independently by two group members. Inter-rater agreement measured (Cohen's κ). Disagreements resolved by structured discussion. Final scores averaged.

All raw scores, agent outputs, and debate transcripts published as supplementary material for reproducibility.

Honest Limitations

What we know we cannot fully solve

A paper that knows its limits is more credible than one that pretends it has none. These are documented upfront, not buried in footnotes.

⚠ Look-ahead bias

Our LLMs were trained on data that includes post-event analyses of 9/11 and Iraq War. They are not truly predicting — they are pattern-matching against training data that includes the outcome.

Mitigation: Use the April 2025 US-China tariff event (very recent, less post-event literature in training data). Acknowledge explicitly. Frame as proof-of-concept.

⚠ Model overlap

Claude, GPT-4o, and Gemini all trained on largely overlapping internet data. Diversity may be smaller than assumed for well-documented events.

Mitigation: Measure initial position variance across agents before debate. Report it. If variance is low, acknowledge it as a finding about model homogenisation.

⚠ Non-stationarity

A 9/11-scale shock in 2001 hit markets without algorithmic trading, ETF dominance, or social-media-driven sentiment. The same shock in 2026 transmits differently.

Mitigation: Weight recent events more heavily. Include regime condition in analogue matching to partially account for structural differences.

⚠ LLM non-reproducibility

The same prompt returns different outputs on different runs. Scientific papers require reproducibility.

Mitigation: Temperature = 0 for all calls. Document exact model versions. Run each scenario three times and report all three outputs with mean and variance.

The honest framing for the paper: This paper validates SPAR on historical events where outcomes are known. The practical use case is forward-looking — a risk manager using the system on currently unfolding events. Future work should evaluate real-time deployment with expert benchmark validation.

Research Contributions

Three novel claims

What a peer reviewer needs to see to accept this paper. Each claim is independently testable and directly tied to a gap in the literature.

01

Three-dimensional geopolitical analogue retrieval framework for US equity markets

Matching by shock category × geographic proximity × economic regime — calibrated on GPR, FRED, and S&P 500 data from 1985–2026. First retrieval framework specifically designed for geopolitical stress scenario generation. Extends: Caldara & Iacoviello (2022)

02

Empirical comparison of model-diversity vs. persona-diversity in financial debate

First study to compare five different frontier LLM providers as debate agents against five personas in one model. Runs on five historical events with pre-registered scoring rubric. Answers whether genuine model diversity improves scenario quality over simulated diversity. Extends: Du et al. (2023) · FinDebate (2025)

03

Four-type position-change decomposition as a debate quality metric

Classifies every agent position revision as evidence-based, consensus-pressure, no meaningful update, or unsupported flip. Measures genuine reasoning quality vs. social conformity in multi-agent LLM debate. Solves the open validation problem noted by Soleimani et al. (2025). Solves gap in: Soleimani et al. (2025)

Conclusion

SPAR is not just a better stress test. It is a different kind of thinking about risk.

Traditional models ask what happened before. SPAR asks which specialists would disagree about what is happening now — and what their disagreement tells us about the tail risks the consensus is missing.

One-line pitch

A structured, auditable bridge between unstructured geopolitical shock narratives and portfolio-level risk estimates — using five frontier LLMs in domain-specialist debate, grounded in historical analogues, validated against real S&P 500 outcomes.
